1 Summary

On Thursday 17 September 2026, SWK Ghana and Aequitas Foundation hosted Pitch Ready, a free 90-minute online workshop on crafting and delivering an elevator pitch. It was the fifth edition of SWK Ghana's programmes and our first workshop with Aequitas Foundation.

Sixty-three people registered over twelve days from four countries, and more than 30 joined the session live on SWK Meet, our online meeting platform. Rev'd Akua Buabema Ofori-Boateng, PhD, facilitated and Ben Brown moderated.

The session taught a six-part structure for a 60-second pitch — hook, who you are, the problem, your solution, the value and the ask — and was designed as practice rather than a lecture, with two rounds built in: drafting a pitch from a template, then delivering it to a partner for feedback before refining it. Network problems and technical issues with the Jitsi app caused some participants to drop out during the session. This report sets out who took part, what was taught, what we learned, and what we will do differently.

2 Why this workshop

About one in five working-age Africans is starting a business (Brookings, 2024), yet capital and networks stay out of reach for many young founders — often not for want of a good idea, but for want of a clear one. Investors lean on credentialed trust, which creates a two-speed ecosystem. One practical lever narrows that gap: the ability to explain what you do, clearly and credibly, in the time it takes to ride an elevator.

The workshop treated the pitch as a skill you practise, not a talent you either have or lack — for founders, agripreneurs, students, community leaders and anyone who has to make someone care, fast.

What the session covered

  1. Why the elevator pitch matters. A short, clear summary of who you are and what you offer, for networking events, job interviews, funding and partnership talks, and every “so what do you do?”. Clarity builds trust, a steady pitch shows you know your value, and brevity respects the listener's time.
  2. Anatomy of a great pitch. Six parts in 60 seconds, set out below.
  3. Mistakes to avoid and hooks that land. Five common mistakes, four ways to open, and example pitches from an agri-tech founder, a recent graduate and a freelance consultant.
  4. Exercise 1: craft your pitch. Five minutes alone with a five-part template: who you help, what you help them do, the benefit, how you differ from the alternative, and a call to action.
  5. Exercise 2: pair up and present. Sixty seconds each, timed by a partner, then one thing that landed and one thing to sharpen.
  6. Delivery tips and round two. Rewrite the pitch with that feedback and deliver it again to a new partner, aiming for confident and clear rather than perfect.
  7. Takeaways, next steps and Q&A.

The framework: six parts in 60 seconds

  1. 18 sec
    Hook

    Win attention with the first line.

  2. 27 sec
    Who you are

    Your name and role, briefly.

  3. 310 sec
    The problem

    The gap or need you address.

  4. 415 sec
    Your solution

    What you do about it.

  5. 510 sec
    The value

    Why it matters, or a quick proof point.

  6. 610 sec
    The ask

    The one next step you want.

About 150 words, spoken naturally, fills a minute. Rehearse out loud until it feels familiar rather than memorised.

How partners gave feedback

Feedback checklist used in the pair exercise
ClarityCould a stranger say back what you do?

StructureDid it move from who you are to the problem, the solution and the ask?
ConfidenceA steady pace, eye contact and no filler words?

What to remember

Clear beats clever.
One idea, not everything.
Always end with an ask.
Practice makes it sound natural.

For the week after, participants were asked to practise daily, out loud and against a timer, and to try their pitch on three people, noticing which line makes them lean in.

6 Challenges and lessons

Connectivity and platform stability

Some participants dropped out because of network problems and technical issues with the Jitsi app. The in-session counter showed 19 people connected around the 30-minute mark, below the 30+ who joined.

  • Send a short how-to-join guide and a backup link with the reminder.
  • Invite people on weak connections to join with video off.
  • Test the platform at the expected audience size before the day.
  • Record sessions so anyone who drops out can catch up.

Turning registrations into attendance

Roughly half of registrants joined live, and nine people registered on the day itself.

  • Send WhatsApp reminders the day before and an hour before the start.
  • Keep registration open until the session begins.

Where people come from

Our WhatsApp community brought in three in every four registrations (76%); LinkedIn and personal referrals each brought one in ten.

  • Keep the community at the centre of promotion, and ask partners to share with their own networks.

Reaching more women

Women made up 35% of registrants.

  • Plan targeted outreach through women-led groups and partners for future editions.
